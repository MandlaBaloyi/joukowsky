THE BLIND GRID: WHY REAL-TIME TELEMETRY IS REQUIRED
STATUS: DRAFT v0.1 - for review

The fundamental flaw in South African municipal water infrastructure is that
it operates blindly. Because municipalities lack high-frequency telemetry at
the "last mile" (the distribution network), they rely on reactive
maintenance.

Currently, if a municipal pipe bursts underground, it bleeds treated water
for days or weeks before it is detected through physical inspection or
customer complaints. When utilities attempt to solve issues - such as
abruptly shutting valves to prevent reservoirs from running dry - they
create severe secondary damage.

Rapid valve closures or pump failures cause the water column to stop
suddenly. The kinetic energy of the moving water is instantly converted into
a violent pressure shockwave known as "water hammer". Because ageing
municipal pipes are often brittle or weakened, these pressure spikes (which
can exceed the maximum operating pressure) fracture the network. The goal of
the joukowsky engine is to detect these transient waves in real time,
instantly identifying sudden pressure drops (bursts) or spikes (hammer)
before catastrophic failure cascades through the grid.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
RESEARCH NOTES (added during planning - for review)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. "Blind" is literal, not a metaphor: it is a sampling problem.
   Where networks are monitored at all, pressure and flow loggers and SCADA
   commonly record at intervals of minutes (typically 1-15 min) [VERIFY
   typical SA municipal logging intervals]. A water hammer event in a
   distribution pipe lasts from a fraction of a second to a few tens of
   seconds, and its leading front rises in milliseconds. A logger sampling
   once every 15 minutes will almost never capture it, and if it does,
   will record one meaningless point. The transient is not "missed by a
   poor algorithm" - it is physically absent from the data.
   See 03_sensing_and_signal/why_scada_is_blind.txt for the numbers.

2. How big is the spike? (Joukowsky: dp = rho * a * dV)
   Stopping water flowing at 1 m/s instantaneously:
     - steel / ductile iron / cast iron main (a ~ 1200-1300 m/s):
           dp ~ 998 * 1250 * 1 ~ 1.25 MPa  ~ 12.5 bar  (~127 m head)
     - asbestos-cement main (a ~ 1000 m/s):   ~ 10 bar
     - uPVC (a ~ 350-450 m/s):                ~ 3.5-4.5 bar
     - HDPE (a ~ 250-350 m/s):                ~ 2.5-3.5 bar
   Typical distribution operating pressures are ~3-9 bar. A surge of
   +10 bar on top of that on a 40+ year old cast iron or AC main is a
   plausible fracture mechanism. Worked in 02_physics_of_transients.

3. The surge has a twin: the down-surge.
   The same event produces a negative wave on the other side of the valve
   (or downstream of a tripped pump). If pressure falls to vapour pressure
   the water column separates; when it rejoins, the collapse can produce
   spikes larger than the Joukowsky value. Low/negative pressure also lets
   contaminated groundwater be drawn in through leaks and joints - a public
   health issue as well as a structural one.
   See 02_physics_of_transients/column_separation_and_intrusion.txt.

4. Bursts are transients too.
   A pipe that ruptures suddenly opens a new outflow. That is the mirror
   image of a valve closure: a negative pressure wave radiates from the
   burst in both directions at speed a. Its arrival time at two or more
   synchronised sensors reveals the burst's location. This is why the
   engine needs high-frequency, time-synchronised sensing: it turns the
   burst's own pressure wave into a locator beacon.

5. Transients also cause slow damage.
   Not every surge breaks a pipe. Repeated pressure cycling fatigues
   plastic pipes and loosens joints, and each event may enlarge existing
   leaks (leakage grows with pressure - see pressure_and_leakage.txt).
   Counting and characterising every transient is therefore valuable even
   when nothing breaks: it builds a fatigue history per pipe.

6. South African operating conditions make this worse.
   See the_operating_environment.txt (load-shedding pump trips,
   intermittent supply and refilling, pressure variation at the
   municipal/private boundary).
