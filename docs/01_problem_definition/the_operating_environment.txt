THE OPERATING ENVIRONMENT: WHY SOUTH AFRICAN NETWORKS SEE MORE TRANSIENTS
STATUS: DRAFT v0.1 - for review

A textbook network operates at steady state and experiences occasional,
planned transients. South African networks do not. Several local conditions
generate transients routinely. These are hypotheses to confirm with field
data, but each is grounded in well-understood physics.

1. Load-shedding and grid instability -> pump trips
   When power to a pumping station is cut, pumps run down within seconds.
   Flow on the discharge side decelerates abruptly, producing a down-surge
   (negative pressure wave) that can reach vapour pressure on high points,
   followed by an up-surge when the column rejoins or the check valve
   slams. Power restoration and simultaneous pump restarts produce a
   second set of transients. Scheduled outages occur at known times,
   which is useful: they give the engine labelled, predictable events for
   calibration (see 05_learning_systems).

2. Intermittent supply and refilling
   Many networks are deliberately or involuntarily run intermittently
   (water shedding, reservoir drawdown, supply interruptions). When a
   drained section is refilled, air pockets form and are compressed, and
   the arriving water column strikes closed ends and air valves.
   Refilling transients are known to be among the most violent in
   distribution systems. Draining also creates negative pressure and
   intrusion risk.

3. Emergency valve operations
   Operators close valves quickly to isolate bursts or protect reservoirs.
   Manual gate valves close slowly in the first turns and very fast in the
   last ~10-20% of travel, where most of the flow change happens - so an
   apparently "slow" closure can still be hydraulically rapid.

4. Ageing, mixed-material networks
   Asbestos-cement, cast iron, steel, uPVC and HDPE coexist, often in the
   same zone. Each has a different wave speed (1000-1300 m/s for rigid
   pipes, 250-450 m/s for plastics), so waves partially reflect at every
   material change. Rigid, old pipes see the largest surges and have the
   least remaining strength.

5. The municipal / private boundary
   A commercial site's internal network is hydraulically connected to the
   municipal main through its connection. Surges on the municipal side
   propagate into the site (attenuated by the meter, check valves and
   pressure reducing valves, if present), and the site's own operations
   (fire-pump tests, booster pumps, solenoid valves, rapid-closing taps)
   create internal transients. The engine must distinguish "came from
   outside" from "originated inside" - which is a localisation problem
   (sensor at the boundary sees it first => external).

6. Poor asset records
   Pipe material, diameter, age and even routing are often uncertain.
   Wave speeds therefore cannot simply be looked up; they must be
   estimated from measured events. This is one of the key reasons the
   engine needs to learn.

Implication for the engine
--------------------------
The engine cannot treat transients as rare anomalies. It must expect a
high background rate of "normal" transients (pump starts, valve operations,
demand switching) and classify them, isolating the rare dangerous ones
(bursts, severe surges, column separation). Event classification is
therefore as important as event detection.
