Why this file exists (owner, 2026-09-29)
----------------------------------------
"Most of the water, even in a city, comes from dams... if our engine
focuses on the pipes but misses where the water is coming from, we might
miss something." Correct. Today the engine treats every source as a
RESERVOIR node with a FIXED head. For a burst lasting seconds that is
right; for everything that happens over hours - supply cuts, reservoirs
running low, pipes draining and refilling - it is blind.

1. The supply chain, source to tap (typical South African city)
------------------------------------------------------------------------
  dam (storage) -> river release, canal or raw-water pipeline
    -> treatment works -> bulk mains (gravity or pumped)
    -> command reservoirs and water towers -> distribution network
    -> the site (campus, mall, mine) -> taps
Bulk suppliers run the first half, municipalities the second
[VERIFY which, per metro]. Each link has its own physics and its own
timescale:
  link                    physics                      timescale
  dam, reservoir, tank    storage (volume balance)     hours - months
  river, canal            open-channel flow            minutes - days
  raw / bulk mains        pressurised pipe (ours)      seconds (waves)
                          + slow balance               hours (demand)
  distribution, site      pressurised pipe (ours)      seconds

2. What happens at the source that the pipes feel
------------------------------------------------------------------------
  - Levels change: a reservoir drawn down over the day lowers every
    pressure downstream; the engine's "normal" pressure moves with it.
  - Supply cuts and water shedding: reservoirs run low -> pipes drain ->
    air enters -> when supply returns, the refilling surges are among the
    most violent transients in SA networks
    (01_problem_definition/the_operating_environment.txt, section 2).
  - Pump stations on bulk mains trip with load-shedding (already a class).
  - Missing water: a bulk main that loses water between two meters leaks,
    even when the leak is too small to send a wave we can feel (gap G-12).
Point to keep: the SLOW physics here is fully visible in slow data. A
reservoir level logged every 15 minutes misses nothing a reservoir does
(P2 cuts both ways: never claim what the rate hides, but use every rate
for what it does show).

3. The physics
------------------------------------------------------------------------
(a) Storage (dams, reservoirs, tanks) - one number per tank:
        A(h) dh/dt = Q_in - Q_out
    with the level-area curve A(h) of the tank or dam basin. Outlets:
    valves (orifice law, as our valves), spillways (weir law
    Q = C_w L H^1.5 [VERIFY C_w for typical crests]). Simple, exact.

(b) Open channels (canals, rivers) - 1-D Saint-Venant equations:
        dA/dt + dQ/dx = q_lateral
        dQ/dt + d(Q^2/A)/dx + g A dy/dx = g A (S0 - Sf)
        Sf = n^2 Q|Q| / (A^2 R^(4/3))       (Manning, n per channel)
    A = wetted area, y = depth, R = hydraulic radius, S0 = bed slope.
    Surface waves travel at c = V +/- sqrt(g y): about 3-10 m/s, not
    1000 m/s. Flow is subcritical (Froude < 1, the usual case) or
    supercritical (steep chutes); hydraulic jumps where it switches.
    Numerics in common use [VERIFY]: the Preissmann four-point implicit
    scheme (the classic for rivers and canals; stable with large time
    steps), MOC for Saint-Venant, finite-volume Godunov schemes for
    shocks and dam-break waves.

(c) Pipes flowing PARTLY full (sewers, and water mains while draining or
    refilling) obey the same Saint-Venant equations with a circular
    cross-section - and switch to pressurised flow when full (mixed
    flow; the "Preissmann slot" trick makes one solver do both
    [VERIFY]). This is exactly the physics of refilling after a supply
    cut (gap G-14): the open-channel work and the refilling surges are
    the same problem.

4. How it connects to our pipe engine
------------------------------------------------------------------------
Timescales differ a thousandfold, so the coupling is loose and clean:
  - the source model (storage, channels) runs in seconds-to-minutes steps
    and hands the pipe network a slowly varying HEAD at each source node
    (reservoir level h(t)) and takes back the flow drawn;
  - the pipe MOC runs in millisecond steps, with that head as a boundary
    that is constant within any one transient;
  - volume is exchanged at the interface, so water is conserved from dam
    to tap (P1).
Both run side by side in the same engine - two solvers, one network
model, each doing the physics it is built for.

5. Data and sensors
------------------------------------------------------------------------
  - Reservoir and tank levels: level loggers (pressure or radar), usually
    in the bulk supplier's or municipality's SCADA (Tier 0 - enough here).
  - Flows: meters on bulk mains and reservoir outlets.
  - Dam levels: published regularly by the national Department of Water
    and Sanitation [VERIFY: frequency, format, licence].
  - Canal and river levels and flows: gauging stations [VERIFY sources].
  No new hardware is needed for the slow physics: it is visible in the
  data utilities already collect.

6. What the engine would gain
------------------------------------------------------------------------
  - Pressure "normal" that follows the source (fewer false alarms).
  - Supply-cut warnings: a reservoir heading empty means pressure will
    fall, pipes will drain and later refill violently - warn before.
  - Refilling surges simulated and classified (gap G-14).
  - Water balance from source to tap: where water goes missing (G-12),
    including small leaks on big mains that no wave reveals.
