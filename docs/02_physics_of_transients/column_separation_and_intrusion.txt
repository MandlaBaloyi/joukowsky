COLUMN SEPARATION, CAVITATION AND INTRUSION
STATUS: DRAFT v0.1 - for review

1. The down-surge
-----------------
Every transient has a low-pressure side: downstream of a closing valve,
downstream of a tripped pump, and at the burst itself. If the absolute
pressure falls to the vapour pressure of water, the liquid column
separates and a vapour cavity forms.

  Vapour pressure of water at 20 C: ~2.34 kPa (absolute).
  The gauge-pressure floor therefore depends on atmospheric pressure,
  i.e. on ALTITUDE:
    - Coast (Durban, Cape Town), p_atm ~ 101 kPa:
        floor ~ (2.3 - 101.3) kPa ~ -99 kPa  ~ -10.1 m head gauge
    - Highveld (Johannesburg/Pretoria ~1400-1750 m), p_atm ~ 83-86 kPa:
        floor ~ -81 to -84 kPa ~ -8.3 to -8.6 m head gauge
  [VERIFY local atmospheric pressures; standard atmosphere gives
  ~83 kPa at 1700 m]
  => On the highveld, cavitation begins at a smaller down-surge than at
  the coast. The engine must know site altitude.

2. Cavity collapse
------------------
When the reflected wave returns, the cavity collapses and the two water
columns collide. The resulting spike can EXCEED the Joukowsky pressure for
the original velocity change, and several collapse cycles can follow.
This is a known, major cause of pipe failure following pump trips.
(Review: Bergant, Simpson & Tijsseling 2006.)

Signature in measured data (useful for classification):
  - pressure flat-lined at the vapour floor for a period (sensor reads its
    minimum plateau),
  - followed by a short, very sharp positive spike,
  - repeated with decreasing amplitude, with timing that does NOT follow
    the regular 4L/a period of an elastic oscillation.

Models:
  - DVCM (discrete vapour cavity model): cavities allowed at MOC nodes.
    Simple, widely used; can produce unrealistic spikes.
  - DGCM (discrete gas cavity model): small gas volume at nodes; more
    stable and realistic for systems with dissolved/free air.
The engine should start with DGCM for simulation of down-surges and
flag any event where simulated or measured head reaches the floor.

3. Negative pressure and contaminant intrusion
----------------------------------------------
A leak is a two-way orifice. During negative-pressure transients, water
(and whatever it carries) outside the pipe can be drawn in through leaks,
cracks and joints. Pipes in SA often run near sewers, and trench water is
frequently contaminated. Field studies in the US measured negative
pressure transients in distribution systems and associated them with
intrusion risk (LeChevallier et al. 2003; Kirmeyer et al. 2001 [VERIFY]).

Implications for the engine:
  - Every event where any sensor (or the simulated field between sensors)
    goes below ~0 bar gauge is a WATER QUALITY event, not just a
    structural one. Log duration x depth ("negative pressure exposure").
  - This is a strong value case for hospitals, food & beverage plants,
    and any site where water quality is critical.

4. Air valves and air pockets
-----------------------------
Air valves admit air at high points when pressure goes negative (limiting
the down-surge) but then must expel it; rapid expulsion can slam the valve
and create a secondary surge. Trapped air pockets, compressed by an
arriving column during refilling, can produce very high pressures. Both
are common in intermittently supplied networks. Model these as gas
cavities with orifice in/out-flow laws.
