GOVERNING EQUATIONS AND THE METHOD OF CHARACTERISTICS (MOC)
STATUS: DRAFT v0.1 - for review

1. The 1-D water hammer equations
---------------------------------
For a pipe with axial coordinate x, head H(x,t) and flow Q(x,t):

  Continuity (mass + wall/fluid elasticity):
      dH/dt + (a^2 / (g A)) * dQ/dx = 0

  Momentum:
      dQ/dt + g A * dH/dx + f Q|Q| / (2 D A) = 0

(Partial derivatives. Convective terms V dV/dx, V dH/dx are dropped
because V << a; slope term absorbed into H as piezometric head.)

This is a hyperbolic system of two PDEs. Its characteristic speeds are
+a and -a: information travels along the pipe in both directions at the
wave speed. That is the mathematical reason time-of-arrival localisation
works.

2. Method of characteristics
----------------------------
Along the lines dx/dt = +a (C+) and dx/dt = -a (C-) the PDEs reduce to
ordinary differential equations. Discretise the pipe into N reaches of
length dx and choose dt = dx / a (Courant number 1: exact for the
frictionless part, no numerical diffusion). With
      B = a / (g A)                  (characteristic impedance)
      R = f dx / (2 g D A^2)         (friction coefficient per reach)
for interior node P at the new time, with neighbours A (left) and B
(right) at the old time:

      C+ :  H_P = C_P - B Q_P,   C_P = H_A + B Q_A - R Q_A |Q_A|
      C- :  H_P = C_M + B Q_P,   C_M = H_B - B Q_B + R Q_B |Q_B|

      =>    H_P = (C_P + C_M) / 2
            Q_P = (C_P - C_M) / (2 B)

(Wylie & Streeter; Chaudhry.) Each boundary supplies one extra equation to
close the one characteristic available there:
  - reservoir:            H_P = H_res
  - dead end:             Q_P = 0
  - valve (orifice law):  Q_P = tau(t) * Q0 * sqrt(dH_valve / dH0)
                          tau(t) = relative opening, from valve curve
  - junction:             sum of Q = 0 and common H (with minor losses)
  - pump:                 pump curve + rotor inertia (for trip/run-down)
  - leak/burst:           Q_L = Cd A_L sqrt(2 g H) (or pressure-dependent
                          leakage law, see pressure_leakage_and_fatigue.txt)
  - air valve, surge tank, PRV: device-specific models.

3. Networks
-----------
Real networks have pipes of different lengths; with one global dt, each
pipe needs an integer number of reaches, so wave speeds are adjusted
slightly (typically <= a few %) or interpolation is used. Wave speed
adjustment is preferable (no numerical damping) but must be recorded as a
known model error.

4. Why the engine needs a fast MOC solver
-----------------------------------------
The solver is used in at least five places:
  (1) Simulate "what would a burst at x look like at the sensors?" for the
      location step (template/likelihood generation).
  (2) Inverse transient analysis - thousands to millions of forward runs
      inside an optimiser or sampler.
  (3) Safe-action check: simulate a proposed valve closure before
      recommending it.
  (4) Generating synthetic training data for classifiers.
  (5) Digital twin: running alongside the real network, fed with measured
      boundary conditions, to compare predicted vs measured.
Therefore it must be fast, deterministic and testable against analytical
solutions. Candidate implementation: a compiled core (Rust or C++) with
Python bindings for research; vectorised over many scenarios at once.
[Decision pending - see 06_engine_architecture]

5. Beyond classical MOC (research track)
----------------------------------------
- Frequency-domain / transfer-matrix models: represent each pipe as a
  2x2 transfer matrix in frequency; fast for small-amplitude (linearised)
  transients; basis of frequency-response leak detection.
- Unsteady friction and viscoelastic extensions (friction_and_damping.txt).
- Two-phase / cavity models (column_separation_and_intrusion.txt).
- Adjoint of the MOC scheme: gives gradients of a data-misfit with respect
  to all parameters in one backward pass -> gradient-based inverse
  transient analysis at scale. Alternatively, write the solver in a
  differentiable framework (JAX / PyTorch) so gradients come for free.
  This is one of the more novel directions available to us.

6. Pressure-reducing valves (added 2026-09-30)
----------------------------------------------
A PRV holds the head just downstream at its setting H_set (in files: the
downstream node's elevation + the pressure setting) when the head
upstream is higher; South African utilities use them everywhere to cut
leakage by lowering night pressure. Three states (as EPANET 2.2's
prvstatus, [VERIFY] against its source):
  ACTIVE  H_down = H_set; the flow is whatever the zone below draws.
          -> OPEN   if H_up < H_set (it cannot reduce what is not there)
          -> CLOSED if the flow would run backwards
  OPEN    a fully open valve: H_up - H_down = R_open Q|Q|,
          R_open = K / (2 g A_valve^2) (K = the valve's minor loss).
          -> ACTIVE if H_down > H_set;  -> CLOSED if the flow reverses
  CLOSED  no flow (it also acts as a check valve).
          -> ACTIVE if H_up > H_set > H_down;  -> OPEN if H_set > H_up > H_down
Steady solve (steady/gga.rs): the valve node is split into its two
sides; ACTIVE fixes the downstream side's head and carries, through a
zero-conductance link, the flow the downstream side sends on (updated each
Newton round); OPEN is a friction link; CLOSED is no link. States are
settled with the check valves, then solved again, until nothing changes.
Transients (moc/network/solver.rs): a real PRV is hydraulically piloted
and needs seconds to move [VERIFY: typical response 2-10 s]; a pressure
wave crosses a site in well under a second. So in a transient the valve
is FROZEN at its steady opening: an orifice with
  R = (H_up - H_down) / Q0^2   (from the steady state)
between the two pipes, never passing flow backwards. With the upstream
C+ (H_u = Cp - B_u Q) and downstream C- (H_d = Cm + B_d Q):
  R Q^2 + (B_u + B_d) Q - (Cp - Cm) = 0,  Q >= 0.
A small wave dH_i arriving from upstream passes on as
  dH_t = 2 B_d dH_i / (B_u + B_d + 2 R Q0)
- a PRV passing flow lets part of a wave through and reflects the rest;
a closed one reflects it all (the zone below it hears nothing). Checked
in V-A10. Limits: the valve's own response over seconds (it opens to
feed a burst downstream) is not modelled - a burst below a PRV draws
more than the frozen opening gives after the first seconds; a PRV closed
at the start stays closed. Gap G-19.

Same day, the other EPANET control valves (boundary/control_valve.rs,
one node kind with four modes; V-A11):
  PSV (pressure-sustaining) holds the head just UPSTREAM at least at its
      setting - the mirror of a PRV (its fixed-head side is upstream; it
      needs no throttling once the head downstream is above the setting);
  FCV (flow-control) passes at most its setting's flow: ACTIVE is a link
      of fixed flow; if that would need the head to rise across it, it
      opens fully and passes what it can;
  TCV (throttle) is a fixed loss R Q|Q| both ways (K from the file).
PRV, PSV and FCV are one-way. All are frozen orifices in transients (a TCV
at its own R). PBV and GPV (rare) stay plain connections.

Valves that move (2026-10-01, V-A13): a valve may be given a response time
tau. Each step its conductance c (Q = c sqrt(dH)) relaxes towards the
conductance that would restore its setting at that moment (from the two
characteristics: the PRV's head below on its setting, the PSV's head above,
the FCV's flow), c <- c + (1 - exp(-dt/tau)) (c_req - c), within 0..fully
open. A one-parameter behavioural model in the spirit of Prescott &
Ulanicki (J. Hydraul. Eng. 129(10), 2003); published pilot-operated PRVs
settle in seconds to tens of seconds (one reported test 7-16 s) [VERIFY
tau per valve: maker data or a recorded pressure step]. No pilot dead time
is modelled. Unknown tau (files never give it): frozen, as before.
