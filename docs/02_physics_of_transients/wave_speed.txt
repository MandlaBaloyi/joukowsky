WAVE SPEED: THE PARAMETER EVERYTHING DEPENDS ON
STATUS: DRAFT v0.1 - for review

Why it matters
--------------
The wave speed a sets (1) the surge magnitude (dp = rho a dV), (2) the
time scale of every event (2L/a), and (3) the conversion from arrival-time
difference to distance in burst location (x = (L + a dt)/2). A 10% error in
a is a 10% error in surge estimate and can put a burst tens of metres away
from where it really is. Knowing a per pipe is central.

Korteweg-Joukowsky formula (thin-walled elastic pipe)
-----------------------------------------------------
        a = sqrt( (K / rho) / (1 + c1 * K D / (E e)) )
  K   = bulk modulus of water ~ 2.19 GPa (20 C)
  rho = 998 kg/m^3
  D   = internal diameter, e = wall thickness
  E   = Young's modulus of the pipe wall
  c1  = restraint factor (~1 for pipe with expansion joints;
        1 - nu^2 for pipe anchored against axial movement; buried pipe
        is somewhere in between). Poisson nu ~ 0.3 metals, ~0.45 plastics.
Wave speed in unbounded water: sqrt(K/rho) = sqrt(2.19e9/998) ~ 1481 m/s.

Worked examples (c1 = 1; our calculations - typical dimensions assumed)

Steel             300     8     200      0.41     ~1250
Ductile iron      150     6     170      0.32     ~1290
Cast iron         150    10     100      0.33     ~1285
Asbestos-cement   150    12      24      1.14     ~1010
uPVC              110   5.3     3.0     15.2      ~ 370
HDPE PE100 SDR11  110    10     ~1.0*   24.1      ~ 295

The things that change wave speed in the real world
---------------------------------------------------
1. Free air / gas. A tiny volume fraction of free air drastically lowers a
   (Wood's equation for a bubbly mixture). ~0.1% free air by volume at a
   few bar can halve the wave speed of a rigid pipe [VERIFY magnitude with
   Wylie & Streeter]. Intermittent supply and refilling leave air in the
   network - so a in SA networks may be lower and more variable than the
   formula suggests. It also depends on pressure (air compresses), so a is
   not a constant even within one pipe.

2. Wall condition. Corrosion and wall thinning (lower e), graphitisation of
   cast iron, and leaching/softening of AC reduce effective stiffness and
   therefore a. This is an OPPORTUNITY: a measured drop in a for a pipe,
   over months, is a non-invasive indicator of deterioration (transient-
   based pipe condition assessment; see references: Stephens, Gong et al.).

3. Restraint, soil and joints. Buried pipes, anchoring and flexible joints
   change c1 and the effective stiffness.

4. Temperature. K of water and E of plastics depend on temperature; plastic
   pipe wave speeds vary noticeably between summer and winter.

5. Viscoelasticity (PE, PVC). The wall keeps deforming after the pressure
   changes (retarded strain). Effect: extra damping and dispersion - wave
   fronts smear out as they travel. Pure elastic models over-predict the
   later oscillations in plastic pipes. Commercial sites often have plastic
   internal networks, so this matters for our first deployments.

How the engine should handle wave speed
---------------------------------------
- Start: prior per pipe from material/diameter using Korteweg, with wide
  uncertainty (e.g. +/- 20%) because asset records are unreliable.
- Update: every event with a known source (scheduled valve operation,
  pump start, deliberate test) gives travel times between sensors ->
  Bayesian update of a per pipe (05_learning_systems).
- Active calibration: deliberately generate small, safe transients (e.g. a
  controlled quick opening of a small hydrant/test valve) to measure a.
- Monitor: track a over time per pipe as a health indicator.
