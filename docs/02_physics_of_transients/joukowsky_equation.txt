THE JOUKOWSKY EQUATION
STATUS: DRAFT v0.1 - for review

1. Statement
------------
A sudden change in velocity dV of a liquid column produces a pressure
change
        dp = - rho * a * dV            (in pressure)
        dH = - (a / g) * dV            (in head)
travelling away from the disturbance at the wave speed a. The sign depends
on direction: for a valve at the downstream end, slowing the flow (dV < 0)
raises pressure upstream of the valve and lowers it downstream.

In terms of flow, with A the pipe cross-sectional area:
        dH = - (a / (g A)) * dQ = - B * dQ,     B = a / (g A)
B is the characteristic impedance of the pipe [s/m^2]. It appears again in
the method of characteristics.

2. Derivation sketch (momentum on the wave front)
-------------------------------------------------
Consider a valve closing instantaneously. In time dt the front moves a
distance a*dt upstream. The fluid in that slice (mass rho*A*a*dt) is
brought from V to V+dV. The pressure force needed is the momentum change
per unit time:
    (dp) * A = - rho * A * a * dV    =>    dp = - rho * a * dV
(Neglects the small correction from the fluid moving at V relative to the
front, i.e. assumes V << a, which holds: V ~ 1 m/s, a ~ 10^3 m/s.)

3. Validity
-----------
Full Joukowsky pressure is reached only if the change is "rapid":
    Tc <= 2L / a
where Tc is the effective closure time and L the distance to the first
significant reflecting boundary (reservoir, large main, open junction).
For slower closures the reflected relief wave returns before closure ends.
A useful first estimate for Tc > 2L/a (Michaud, linear flow decrease):
    dH_max ~ 2 L V0 / (g Tc)
Joukowsky is the upper bound for a single, clean velocity change - but NOT
an absolute upper bound in real systems. It can be exceeded by:
  - column separation and cavity collapse (see column_separation...)
  - reflections superposing (dead ends double the wave: R = +1)
  - line packing in long pipes (friction effect)
  - trapped air pockets being compressed during refilling.

4. Worked numbers
-----------------
(a) Valve closure, V0 = 1.0 m/s stopped instantaneously:
      steel/DI/CI, a ~ 1250 m/s:  dH = 1250 * 1.0 / 9.81 ~ 127 m ~ 12.5 bar
      AC,          a ~ 1000 m/s:  dH ~ 102 m ~ 10 bar
      uPVC,        a ~  370 m/s:  dH ~  38 m ~ 3.7 bar
      HDPE,        a ~  300 m/s:  dH ~  31 m ~ 3.0 bar
    (wave speeds derived in wave_speed.txt)

(b) Time scale of the event:
      L = 200 m,  a = 1250 m/s:  2L/a = 0.32 s   period 4L/a = 0.64 s
      L = 1000 m, a = 1250 m/s:  2L/a = 1.6 s    period 4L/a = 3.2 s
      L = 200 m,  a = 370 m/s:   2L/a = 1.08 s   period 4L/a = 2.16 s
    So a "rapid" closure in a campus-scale network means closing in well
    under a second or two - achievable with solenoid valves, quarter-turn
    ball/butterfly valves, and the last turns of a gate valve.

5. The burst as an "inverse valve"
----------------------------------
A pipe bursting at an interior point suddenly withdraws flow Q_L. The burst
point is a junction of two pipe halves, each of impedance B, so seen from
the burst the network impedance is B/2 (two paths in parallel). A negative
wave of magnitude
        dH = - (B / 2) * Q_L = - a Q_L / (2 g A)
radiates in BOTH directions. Inverting:
        Q_L ~ 2 g A |dH| / a
This is how the engine QUANTIFIES a burst from the measured step (P4).

Example: DN150 AC main, A = pi * 0.075^2 = 0.01767 m^2, a = 1000 m/s,
B = 1000 / (9.81 * 0.01767) ~ 5770 s/m^2.
  Q_L = 2 l/s (0.002 m^3/s)  -> dH ~ 5770/2 * 0.002 ~ 5.8 m ~ 0.57 bar
  Q_L = 10 l/s               -> dH ~ 29 m ~ 2.8 bar (upper bound: real
                                bursts open over finite time and the leak
                                flow falls as pressure falls)
A 2 l/s burst loses ~173 m^3/day. Its wave is ~0.6 bar at the source -
easily measurable with a 0.1%FS 16 bar transducer (resolution ~0.016 bar)
if the sensor samples fast enough and the wave is not too attenuated.

6. Waves at junctions and material changes
------------------------------------------
When a wave of amplitude F in pipe 1 reaches a junction of pipes
1..n (impedances B_i = a_i / (g A_i)), the transmitted and reflected
amplitudes are:
        T = 2 (1/B_1) / sum_i (1/B_i)       transmitted into every pipe
        R = T - 1                           reflected back into pipe 1
Special cases:
  - n identical pipes (tee: n = 3):  T = 2/3, R = -1/3
  - closed/dead end:                  R = +1 (pressure doubles)
  - large reservoir:                  R = -1 (wave inverts)
  - PVC pipe joined to a steel pipe of equal bore: B_steel/B_PVC ~ 3.4;
    a wave from steel into PVC: T = 2 (1/B_s) / (1/B_s + 1/B_p) ~ 0.46.
Consequences for the engine:
  - Each tee roughly divides the front by 1.5; waves decay quickly in
    dense, branched networks. Sensor spacing must account for this.
  - Reflections produce secondary fronts that arrive later; the location
    algorithm uses the FIRST arrival and treats reflections as extra
    information (or noise) rather than new events.
  - Reflection timing is itself informative: it encodes distances to
    features (dead ends, material changes, closed valves, existing leaks).

Implementation note: burst size in the alert (2026-09-30)
--------------------------------------------------------
crates/domain/localisation/src/size.rs inverts the front model: each
logger's measured drop d_i = F_i Q, F_i = the first front per unit flow at
the located point ((B/2) x junction transmissions x plastic damping), so
Q = sum(F d / s^2) / sum(F^2 / s^2), with noise and wave-speed doubt
(pipeline speed_margin, 10%: B is proportional to a) as its uncertainty,
and a misfit that flags loggers disagreeing (an echo in the window).
Located bursts carry it (Alert.size, the text summary and the viewer).
Pipeline scenario (a 10 mm hole on the estate, 128 S/s loggers, 2 cm
noise): "about 1.55 l/s (+/- 0.16)" - the hole let out 1.55 l/s. A burst
that opens slowly gives smaller fronts: the size is then a lower bound.
